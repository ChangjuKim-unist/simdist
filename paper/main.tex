\title{Identify, Then Cover: Where Coverage Comes From\\in Simulation-Pretrained World-Model Planners}

\begin{abstract}
Simulation-pretrained latent world models with sampling-based planners are adapted to real legged robots by finetuning the latent dynamics on minutes of real data. We measure, component by component, what such adaptation can fix. In a simulation-to-simulation benchmark on a Unitree Go1 (perturbed actuator strength, payload and friction; 18 robots $\times$ 100\,s per condition; paired bootstrap intervals), the dynamics regimes a system can handle are decided by the planner's sampling prior: when the prior's randomization does not reach the regime, falls under a $0.4\times$ torque limit stay at about 10 per 1000 steps whether or not the planner runs and whether or not the dynamics is adapted on 45k steps of data, while a prior randomized over that regime brings them to about 2 without real data. Inside its coverage, planning lowers the prior's falls by 2--5$\times$; outside it, planning does not help. Supervised updates of the dynamics or of the prior on data collected by a different prior are harmful (2.2$\times$ and 2.8$\times$ more falls than no update). The covering prior does not identify the actuator regime from its history, but replaying logged transitions in the simulator identifies it regardless of which policy collected the logs ($0.45$--$0.50$ for a true $0.40$). We therefore propose to spend real data on identification rather than model fitting: identify the actuator scale from logs, train the prior in simulation to cover it, plan on top, and adapt the model only on on-policy data from the identified simulator. We report the simulation evidence, the pre-registered hypotheses with their outcomes, and the hardware protocol under software torque caps with known ground truth.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Sim-to-real pipelines for world-model planners pretrain a latent model $(E,C,f,R,V,\pi)$ in simulation and, on the robot, finetune only the latent dynamics $f$ from a few minutes of data while the encoder, reward, value and policy prior stay frozen~\cite{levy2026simdist}. Whether such a system can handle a condition outside its pretraining distribution is then attributed to the adaptation and to the planner. This paper measures that attribution component by component. Using the public SimDist pipeline on a Unitree Go1 and a simulation-to-simulation benchmark in which the ``real'' robot is a perturbed copy of the training simulator, we measure, for each component, what it can fix.

We call a dynamics regime \emph{covered} by a prior when the prior alone keeps the fall rate within twice its nominal rate. Our central claim is that coverage is decided at pretraining by the prior's randomization and cannot be bought later: adapting $f$ on 45k steps of data or planning on top of the pretrained model leaves falls under a weak actuator unchanged unless the prior was randomized over that regime. Planning is valuable for a different reason: it corrects the compounding error of the behavior-cloned prior inside its coverage. Real data is harmful in a specific way: a supervised update of the model or of the prior degrades the deployed system when the data were collected by a different prior, as the concentrability term of batch system identification predicts~\cite{ross2012agnostic}.

These measurements lead to a recipe. The actuator regime is not recoverable from the prior's observation history, because the covering expert never saturates its motors, but it is recoverable by replaying logged transitions through the simulator, which does not depend on which policy produced the logs. Real data should therefore be used to \emph{identify} the physics; the prior should be trained in simulation to \emph{cover} the identified regime; the planner runs on top; and model adaptation, if used, should take on-policy data generated in the identified simulator. Our contributions are (1) a component-wise measurement of what adaptation, planning and the prior fix, with pre-registered hypotheses and their outcomes (Table~
ef{tab:hyp}); (2) the coupling result for model and prior updates; (3) a policy-independent replay identifier for the actuator axis and the identify-then-cover recipe (Algorithm~
ef{alg:itc}); (4) a hardware protocol with ground-truth actuator weakening (software torque caps) on the Go1.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Sim-pretrained world-model planners.} SimDist~\cite{levy2026simdist} and related real-world model-based systems~\cite{wu2022daydreamer,hansen2024tdmpc2,hafner2023dreamerv3,feng2023fowm} adapt a latent model from minutes of data; none studies how the pretraining randomization of the planner's prior bounds what adaptation can fix. \textbf{Robust and adaptive locomotion.} Privileged distillation with domain randomization~\cite{kumar2021rma,long2024him,smith2023walk,ma2024dreureka} yields policies that either identify the regime from history or are robust to it; we measure which of the two a motor-randomized prior does and find robustness. \textbf{Policy-dependent model error.} Batch system identification carries a concentrability factor between the deploying and collecting policies~\cite{ross2012agnostic}; model-based RL analyses~\cite{janner2019mbpo,rajeswaran2020game,wang2023pdml,lambert2020objective} reach the same conclusion. We provide the first hardware-relevant measurement of this coupling for a latent world model. \textbf{Identification from real data.} Fitting the randomization distribution to real rollouts~\cite{chebotar2019simopt,ramos2019bayessim,tiboni2023dropo,tiboni2024doraemon} and active system identification~\cite{memmel2024asid,sobanbabu2025spiactive} are established for policies; our replay identifier is DROPO's likelihood restricted to the torque-binding steps, and our contribution is not the identifier but the finding that, for a world-model planner, identification is the right use of real data while model fitting is not. Residual world models~\cite{lanier2025redraw} are a complementary way to limit what adaptation can overwrite.

\section{Setting and Measurements}
\textbf{System.} We use SimDist's Go1 pipeline: a latent world model with history encoder, latent dynamics, reward, value and a behavior-cloned policy head $\pi$; MPPI with 200 samples $\times$ 3 iterations at 50\,Hz, 5\,\% of the samples drawn from the prior; real-data adaptation finetunes $f$ only. The sim-to-sim benchmark perturbs the simulator (motor-strength scale $s$ applied to the torque limit, payload, foot friction); 45k real-format steps are collected with the deployed planner and used for adaptation exactly as on the robot. Performance is measured on 18 robots $\times$ 100\,s per condition (falls per 1000 steps, reward per step) with paired bootstrap 95\,\% intervals.

\textbf{Priors and arms.} Besides SimDist's narrow prior (nominal-condition expert), we train PPO experts with motor-strength randomization $\mathcal{U}(w, 1.1)$, $w\in\{0.9,0.7,0.5,0.3\}$ (one seed for $w\in\{0.9,0.7\}$, three seeds for $w\in\{0.5,0.3\}$), and clone each into a history-conditioned MLP prior with an identical recipe (1.5M windows, 20k steps). Arm D executes the prior alone (receding horizon); arm B0 runs MPPI with that prior on the pretrained model; B adds dynamics adapted on 45k steps collected by SimDist's narrow prior; B$'$ adapts on 45k steps collected by the deployed prior. For a comparison with SimDist's own, much larger prior we also retrain only the policy head of the pretrained world model on 7.7M steps of pretraining-style data from the $w=0.3$ expert (``broad head'').

Table~\ref{tab:cov} sweeps the prior's randomization width at a true scale of $0.4$. Falls stay near 10 per 1000 steps until the randomization reaches the regime and drop to $2.6$ when it does. The planner on the pretrained model does not move this curve; outside the coverage it raises falls slightly but significantly ($1.13$, $1.14$). Adapting the dynamics on 45k steps collected by the deployed planner does not change it either (B$'$ vs.\ B0: $1.00$ [0.84, 1.19] with the $w=0.3$ prior). With SimDist's own policy head the picture is the same: SimDist's planner falls $4.1$ times per 1000 steps at $0.4\times$ torque, the broad head $1.5$, with no real data. Widening is not free for a well-trained head: nominal reward drops by 22\,\% and falls at friction $0.15$ double ($1.0\to2.2$); a prior buys the axis it was randomized on.

\subsection{Planning recovers the prior's imperfection}
Inside the coverage, the planner lowers the falls of every cloned prior at nominal and $0.6\times$ torque (B0/D between $0.22$ and $0.51$ for $w\ge0.5$, all intervals excluding 1), with the largest gains for the least accurate priors and the smallest for the $w=0.3$ priors ($0.57$--$0.85$ at nominal). Outside the coverage it does not help (Table~\ref{tab:cov}). Two pre-registered planner modifications (annealed sampling noise as in~\cite{xue2025dialmpc}, and a gate that executes the prior's plan when the predicted latent diverges from the encoded one) gave no gain (Table~\ref{tab:hyp}).

Table~\ref{tab:coupling} adapts the dynamics, and separately the prior, on 45k steps collected either by SimDist's narrow prior or by the deployed ($w=0.3$) prior. Data from the other prior degrade both updates by more than $2\times$ relative to no update; data from the deployed prior do not hurt the cloned prior and improve its nominal condition (falls $\times0.74$ [0.55, 0.98]). Weighting the foreign data by the deployed prior's consistency with the logged actions, or weighting the prior's training data by a real-vs-simulation density ratio, reduced but did not remove the harm (ratios $1.4$--$1.7$), and a residual on the frozen dynamics removed it at $0.4\times$ torque but not at nominal: the visited regions overlap too little for re-weighting to help. With the broad head, on-policy adaptation at the true condition is itself harmful (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}), so ``collected by the deployed prior'' is necessary but not sufficient.

\subsection{The regime is invisible to the prior but identifiable by replay}
A probe regressing the motor scale from the prior's 25-step history reaches $R^2=0.004$ on the broad expert's data, and adding the true scale to the history does not improve next-state prediction: the covering expert walks with one gait that never saturates its motors, so its data carry no trace of the scale. The scale is nevertheless identifiable from the deployed system's logs by replay (Algorithm~\ref{alg:itc}, step 1). From 45k-step logs at a true scale of $0.40$ the maximum-a-posteriori estimate is $0.50$ (logs of the deployed prior) and $0.45$ (logs of SimDist's narrow prior, which falls often); from nominal logs the posterior is flat above $0.7$, where the limit never binds, with mean $1.03$. The $0.05$--$0.10$ upward bias is partly explained by the benchmark scaling both the torque limit and the actuator's saturation curve while the identifier scales the limit only; a torque cap on hardware has no such ambiguity. In a joint grid the replay also identifies added payload ($3.0$\,kg for a true $3.0$) but trades it off against motor strength, and it does not identify foot friction, which does not express itself in a 0.4\,s open-loop replay. Identification needs torque-binding steps, as active identification work emphasizes~\cite{memmel2024asid,sobanbabu2025spiactive}; we have not yet measured how few logged steps suffice.

\section{Identify, Then Cover}\label{sec:method}
\begin{figure}[t]
\begin{small}
\noindent\textbf{Algorithm 1: Identify, then cover.}\label{alg:itc}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Identify.} Logs $\{(o_t,a_t)\}$ from the deployed system under the target condition. For candidates $s$ on a grid ($0.2$--$1.2$, step $0.05$; 18 simulated robots each), reset the robot to the logged state (joint positions and velocities, base angular and linear velocity, roll and pitch from the gravity vector, height from leg kinematics), apply the logged actions open-loop for 20 steps (0.4\,s), and score the joint-space prediction error on steps $5$--$20$, weighting each step by $(\max_j|\tau_j|/\tau_{\max})^2$ where $\tau$ is the logged PD torque demand. Over 60 random segments per robot, $\log p(s)\propto -e(s)/(2\epsilon^2)$ with $\epsilon=0.1\,\mathrm{median}_s\,e(s)$; return the MAP $\hat s$ (or the posterior mean when the posterior is flat).
\item \textbf{Cover.} Train the expert with motor randomization $\mathcal{U}(\hat s-\delta,\ \hat s+\delta')$ containing $\hat s$ with margin, generate pretraining-style data, and retrain only the policy head of the pretrained world model (encoder, dynamics, reward, value frozen).
\item \textbf{Plan.} Run the unchanged MPPI on the pretrained model with the new prior.
\item \textbf{Adapt (optional).} Generate on-policy data with the deployed prior in the identified simulator and finetune the latent dynamics on it; do not finetune on real logs.
\end{enumerate}
\end{small}
\end{figure}
Algorithm~1 is a composition of known pieces (offline randomization fitting, privileged distillation, SimDist's planner and adaptation); what is new is the assignment of roles that the measurements force: coverage from the prior, correction from the planner, identification rather than model fitting from real data. The margin in step 2 is currently hand-set ($\delta=0.2$); choosing it from the posterior width is future work, as is the retraining cost (about 2.5\,h for the expert and 2\,h for the head on one consumer GPU per regime).

\textbf{Step 4 in the benchmark.} Adapting the broad head on 45k on-policy steps generated at the identified scale ($\hat s=0.5$) changed falls at the true $0.4$ from $1.49$ to $1.24$ (ratio $0.84$ [0.62, 1.11], not significant) and left the nominal condition unchanged. Adapting it on on-policy data generated at the true scale ($0.4$) raised falls to $2.74$ (ratio $1.84$ [1.25, 2.51]) and degraded the nominal condition ($1.38$ [1.14, 1.60]); removing the windows that end in a fall made it worse ($3.26$). We read this as the frozen value head misjudging latent trajectories once the dynamics has learned saturation, but we cannot yet separate this from the identifier's upward bias, which makes the identified-simulator data fall-free; a control with matched fall content is pending. \todo{P10f: prior trained on $\mathcal{U}(0.3,0.7)$ around $\hat s$ vs.\ the blanket $\mathcal{U}(0.3,1.1)$ prior: results pending.}

\section{Hardware Protocol}
Actuator weakening on the Go1 is realized in the low-level bridge as a host-side PD law saturated at $s\times23.7$\,Nm (the simulator's effort limit), so the ground-truth scale is known. Conditions: flat ground nominal, $s=0.6$, $s=0.4$, and a 3\,kg payload. Models: SimDist with its narrow prior adapted on 29.5\,min of real data; the broad prior alone; the broad prior with the planner on the pretrained model; the same with the model adapted on identified-simulator data; and, as the coupling test, the broad prior with the model adapted on the 29.5\,min collected by the narrow prior. Ten 30\,s runs per model and condition at commanded speeds of 0.3 and 0.6\,m/s; falls (bridge fall latch), speed-tracking error (leg odometry) and distance are logged. Pre-registered predictions, recorded before the sessions: (i) the replay identifier recovers $0.6$ and $0.4$ within $\pm0.15$ from one minute of logs; (ii) at $s=0.4$ the broad prior alone falls less than half as often as SimDist; (iii) the planner's reduction of the broad prior's falls at nominal is at least as large as in simulation ($\times0.85$); (iv) the model adapted on the narrow prior's 29.5\,min degrades the broad prior by at least $1.3\times$. \prelim{Results of the November sessions will replace this paragraph.}

\section{Limitations}
All quantitative results are from a simulation-to-simulation benchmark on one embodiment; two of the five randomization widths have one expert seed, and seed variance is comparable to the width effect for the cloned priors, which is why we also report the retrained policy head. Inside the covered regime a good prior matches the planner, so the value of planning rests on the prior being imperfect, which we expect on hardware but have not yet shown. Replay identification works for the actuator axis and payload but not for friction, and motor strength and payload partially trade off in a joint grid. Adaptation on true-condition data degraded the broad head in our benchmark; we report this as an observation, not a general claim, and the amount of logged data the identifier needs is not yet measured. No hardware results are reported at this stage.

\section{Conclusion}
In a simulation-pretrained world-model planner, the pretraining prior decides which dynamics regimes the robot can handle, planning repairs the prior inside that envelope, and supervised updates on real data are tied to the policy that collected it. We propose to spend real data on identifying the actuator regime and to buy coverage in simulation; the hardware stage tests this under torque caps with known ground truth.
